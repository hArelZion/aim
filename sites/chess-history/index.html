The rooms

How the game travelled.

Every culture that adopted chess rewrote it a little. Walk through in order.

Ic. 600 · India
Chaturanga

An army on a board

In the Gupta empire, a game called chaturanga — “four divisions” — set infantry, cavalry, elephants and chariots against each other on an eight-by-eight grid. Win by capturing the raja.

The four divisions became the pawn, knight, bishop and rook you still play with.

A rule from the very first boards survives intact: the pawn moves one square forward and captures diagonally, like a foot soldier with a spear held across his body.

IIc. 650 · Persia
Shatranj

A courtly pastime

When the game reached the Sasanian court it became shatranj. After the Arab conquest of Persia it spread across the Islamic world, where masters such as al-Adli and as-Suli wrote the first books of openings and problems.

“Shah mat” — the king is helpless — is where our word checkmate comes from.

IIIc. 1000 · Europe
Into Christendom

Kings, bishops, worried faces

Through Moorish Spain, Sicily and the Viking trade routes, chess reached Europe around the year 1000. The elephant became a bishop, the vizier a queen — still a weak piece that crept one square diagonally.

Chess was so popular among clergy that several church councils tried, and failed, to ban it.

IV1475 · Valencia
The mad queen

The day the game sped up

Some time in the late fifteenth century, in Spain, the queen was given the power to move any distance in any straight line. The bishop got its long diagonal too. Games that had dragged on for hours were suddenly decided in a few dozen moves.

Contemporaries called it ajedrez de la dama, the queen's chess, or more bluntly, scacchi alla rabiosa, mad chess. Within a generation it had swept Europe; the old game simply vanished. Modern chess is barely 550 years old.

V1750–1900 · Paris, London
The café masters

Coffee, smoke and sacrifices

At the Café de la Régence in Paris and Simpson's Divan in London, players gambled, sacrificed pieces with abandon and drew crowds. In 1851 London hosted the first international tournament; in 1886 Steinitz became the first official World Champion.

Philidor, the café's star, also composed operas, and played three blindfold games at once.

VI1948–1990 · USSR
The Soviet school

Chess as a state science

The Soviet Union turned chess into a national project: clubs in every town, trainers in every school, preparation like scientific research. From 1948 until 1972 every world champion was Soviet.

Then, in Reykjavík, an American named Bobby Fischer beat Boris Spassky at the height of the Cold War, and the whole world watched a board game on the evening news.

VII1997 – today
The machines

Deep Blue, and after

In May 1997 IBM's Deep Blue beat the reigning champion Garry Kasparov in a six-game match. Twenty years later AlphaZero taught itself the game in hours and played with a strangely human daring.

Machines didn't kill the game: more people play chess today than at any point in its history.

The object

The Lewis chessmen.

Found in a sand dune on the Isle of Lewis in 1831, these 93 pieces of walrus ivory and whale tooth were probably carved in Norway around 1150. The queens hold their cheeks in dismay; some rooks bite their shields like berserkers.
